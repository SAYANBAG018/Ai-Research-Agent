# 1. Introduction  

Armed conflict is a recurring source of macro‑economic uncertainty.  Because equity markets price expectations about future corporate earnings, fiscal policy, and risk, wars can generate pronounced movements in stock‑market indices, sector returns, and volatility.  Understanding how wars affect markets is valuable for investors, policymakers, and scholars who need to gauge the magnitude, timing, and channels of these effects.  This report synthesises the limited set of empirical studies that were supplied, focusing on three well‑documented patterns that emerge from the available evidence.

# 3. Analysis and Discussion  

## 3.1  Frequency and magnitude of market reactions  

The Bocconi event‑study (Finding 1) suggests that wars do **not** automatically trigger market turmoil; only about one‑third of the 101 conflicts examined produced statistically significant abnormal returns.  This heterogeneity implies that market participants differentiate wars by characteristics such as geographic location, scale, and whether the conflict is internal or interstate.  The same study highlights that **international wars** (i.e., conflicts between sovereign states) generate larger equity‑market responses than internal wars, possibly because interstate wars are more likely to affect trade flows, foreign‑direct investment, and global risk premia.

## 3.2  Short‑run directional bias  

The SIEPR evidence (Finding 2) documents a **large, immediate negative shock** to U.S. equities when a war is announced, with an average decline of roughly 15 %.  The sectoral pattern—consumer‑discretionary, airlines, and IT underperform while gold and energy outperform—reflects a classic “flight‑to‑safety” and commodity‑price channel.  The estimated $10‑per‑barrel rise in oil prices provides a concrete transmission mechanism: higher energy costs depress corporate margins in many sectors, while energy‑related firms benefit.

In contrast, the Verdad Capital analysis (Finding 3) shows **positive short‑run returns** for the UK market during the first ten days after conflict involvement.  The average 2‑3 % rise in broad market indices and the 4 % uplift in defense stocks suggest a “war‑time rally” that may be driven by expectations of increased defence spending, patriotic buying, or reduced uncertainty about the conflict’s outcome once it becomes public
